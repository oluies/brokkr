Brokkr

Could a CUTE-style Chisel tensor engine accelerate quantum simulation? A design assessment, with the gate-fusion measurements that decide it.

17 October 2026 · status: the density-matrix engine runs a full Kraus channel against Scotty · oluies/brokkr

The short version

Yes, the shape transfers — but not for the reason it first appears to. The hot kernel of state-vector simulation is structurally identical to what a tensor engine does: a strided gather, a small dense complex multiply-accumulate, a scatter. Gate fusion turns it into a genuine GEMM.

What fusion does not do is make the work smaller. It is an exchange rate between bandwidth and arithmetic, and the rate depends entirely on how the operators are stored. A dense-only pass trades badly: six-qubit blocks cut traffic to 0.2× but raise flops to 5.9×, and lose to the specialised 2×2 kernel at every width. Storing each operator in the tightest of three forms changes the rate enough to win on a CPU outright — 1.3× on a brickwork circuit and 2.8× on Grover.

That is the useful result, and it is not the one expected. The engine's job is not to run a faster dense kernel; it is to serve a mix of three datapaths in which the dense one is the minority.

And the assessment ends somewhere else again. Measured against the same roofline, a density matrix of ten qubits reaches 683 flop/byte where a fused state vector reaches 15.9 — and it fits on chip, needs no fusion pass, and has no banking problem. A state-vector engine is not CUTE. A density-matrix engine very nearly is.

01What CUTE actually is

Worth pinning down first, because it is not a simulator and its README is one line. From the repository itself:

  • OpenXiangShan/CUTE is “a scalable CPU-centric and ultra-utilized tensor engine for convolutions” (JSA 2024), now at v2.
  • Chisel 6.7.0 on Scala 2.13.15, dual-built with Mill and sbt, with -Ytasty-reader enabled.
  • Submodules: rocket-chip (OpenXiangShan fork), Utility, XSAICache (high-bandwidth L2), CUTEFPE (a C++ floating-point-engine model).
  • src/main/scala is a textbook accelerator: ABMatrixReg and CMatrixReg operand register files; A/B/C DataController, MemoryLoader and MultiChannelsAML on the memory side; MatrixTE and ReducePE for compute; OmegaNetworkRouter and TransposeLoadEngine as the shuffle fabric; plus LocalMMU, TaskController and CuteDifftest.
  • Flow: mill CUTE.test.runMain cute.TestTop --target systemverilog --split-verilog, then vlsi_mem_gen for SRAM macros. Verification is per-unit chiseltest specs plus end-to-end difftest against a software golden model.

So “similar to CUTE” means a Chisel accelerator, CPU-coupled through rocket-chip, with a banked-SRAM memory subsystem, verified unit-by-unit and then differentially against a reference implementation.

02The kernel is already the right shape

Here is the inner loop of Scotty, applying a one-qubit gate:

iterator.foreach: i =>
  val clearedBit = nthCleared(i, index)        // strided address generation
  ...                                          // gather an amplitude pair
  Complex.sum(Complex.product(...), Complex.product(...))   // 2x2 complex matvec
  state(oneIndex) = ...                        // scatter back

Gather at a stride, multiply by a small dense complex matrix, scatter. That is what MatrixTE plus TransposeLoadEngine plus OmegaNetworkRouter do. And nthCleared inserts a zero bit into the amplitude index — qubit addressing is pure bit permutation, which is exactly what an Omega or banyan network is for. The shuffle fabric CUTE needs for convolution is a better fit for quantum state-vector addressing than it is for its own workload.

03Why the naive port starves

“Ultra-utilized” means CUTE is built for a workload with high arithmetic intensity. Convolution reuses each loaded operand many times, so a wide multiplier array stays fed. One-qubit gates have almost no reuse at all.

Per amplitude pair: two complex multiplies and one complex add, counting a complex multiply as six flops and a complex add as two — 28 flops, against 32 bytes read and 32 written in double precision. That is 0.44 flop/byte, roughly twenty to a hundred times below what a systolic array needs. Drop MatrixTE in unchanged and you have built a tensor engine that runs at a few percent utilisation.

Fusing a run of gates that touch the same k qubits into one dense 2k × 2k operator fixes the ratio, because the operator is loaded once and reused across every one of the 2n−k slices of the state. Counting the operator load, the modelled intensity is:

Two things bound k from above here, and neither is the intensity — a third, tighter one appears once the host is measured. The fused operator has to fit in whatever register file or scratchpad feeds the multipliers — 4k complex entries, so 1 MiB of operand SRAM caps k at 8. And building the operator costs a dense 2k-cubed matrix product per gate fused, so it only amortises when n − k is large. Both point at a design window of k = 6 to 8, intensity 16 to 64 flop/byte. That window does not survive the host-cost measurement further down the page, which caps the width at 5 for a register that fits on chip.

The finding

Fusion does not reduce the work. It converts memory traffic into arithmetic, at a fixed and knowable exchange rate. Fusing k one-qubit gates on k distinct qubits divides traffic by k and multiplies flops by 8·2k / 14k — 6.1× at k = 6, which is what the measured 5.9× confirms.

With every operator stored densely, that trade is a loss at every width, and the table shows it: the specialised 2×2 kernel wins outright. The engine only pays off if its flop-to-byte balance exceeds the exchange rate — which remains the one quantitative criterion the project rests on, but the rate itself turns out to be a design variable rather than a constant. The next two sections are about moving it.

Two caveats on these numbers, both of which make the hardware case better rather than worse. The greedy pass densifies operators that did not need densifying: RZ, PHASE, CZ and CPHASE are diagonal, costing one complex multiply per amplitude unfused and a full dense block fused. A sparsity-aware pass that keeps diagonal blocks diagonal would cut the flop inflation substantially. And the fused kernel is a generic d × d loop against a hand-specialised 2×2 one; some of the gap is tuning, not physics.

Grover is the worst case, and it is instructive

Grover’s search is the obvious benchmark, so it is worth saying plainly that the greedy dense pass handles it badly — and why. Both the oracle and the diffusion operator contain a controlled Z over every qubit. That gate is diagonal: the unfused simulator applies it in one pass with a single sign flip per amplitude. Densified, it asks for a 4k-entry operator. At ten qubits that is a 16 MiB operator applied to a 16 KiB state:

Intensity barely moves and the fused path is three to nine times slower, because one wide dense block applied to a single slice dominates everything. Past twelve qubits the pass cannot represent the circuit at all — there is a MaxDenseWidth guard that refuses rather than exhausting memory.

The lesson is not that Grover is a bad benchmark. It is that a dense-only engine is the wrong engine. Diagonal operators — multi-controlled Z, RZ, PHASE, CPHASE — are a large fraction of real circuits and need a cheap path in both the fusion pass and the hardware: one multiplier and a phase table, not a MAC array. Any RTL that only does dense blocks would run Grover slower than the CPU it is attached to.

The form lattice, and what it changed

So the pass no longer stores a dense operator unless it has to. Operators are kept in the tightest of three forms, which is closed under composition — and these are not three storage optimisations, they are three different datapaths:

What the numbers say

The permute path costs what the phase path costs. 19 910 against 20 775 cells per amplitude per cycle — within 4%. Permuting amplitudes is, to within measurement noise, free relative to multiplying them.

The dense path costs 8.5× either of them, which is 2k as predicted, and the ratio grows with k: at the k = 6 design point it would be around 64×.

The shuffle fabric is 1.4% of the permute path — 2 304 cells of 159 287. The thing that looked like the architectural risk is a rounding error next to the multipliers.

So the three-datapath argument holds, but its content has shifted. It is not that each path is differently expensive; it is that two of the three are the same cheap thing and one is expensive. Routing 18 diagonal and 21 monomial blocks of 65 through dense hardware would cost 8.5× what they need, and the saving is throughput: the same silicon runs them eight lanes wide.

The form is decided by reading the matrix rather than from a table of gate names, so it cannot disagree with the semantics and classifies Dagger and user-defined gates for free. Controlled gates inherit their target’s form, which is why CZ, CPHASE and every multi-controlled Z now cost 2k. Dense products are re-classified after multiplication, which catches H-after-H and most Clifford cancellations. And gates wider than the fusion budget are not lowered at all — they become a native op that the simulator applies in one pass, because building any operator for an all-qubit controlled gate, even a cheap one, is the pass making work for itself.

What it bought

Brickwork, 22 qubits. Flop inflation at width 4 falls from 2.2× to 1.4×, and at width 6 from 5.9× to 3.8×; at width 2 the fused circuit now does fewer flops than the unfused one. The form mix at width 6 is 26 dense, 18 diagonal and 21 monomial — 60% of blocks never touch the MAC array.

Grover, 14 qubits — previously not expressible above ten at all. Eight native ops, one per oracle and diffusion operator; largest operator 64 KiB against 16 MiB, a 256× reduction; traffic at 0.3× for only 1.8× the flops.

The corrected conclusion

The earlier statement on this page — that fusion loses on a CPU at every width — was true of a dense-only pass and is false in general. Form-aware fusion wins at widths 2 to 4 on both workloads, peaking at 1.3× and 2.8×, and only becomes a loss past width 5 where the added arithmetic finally outweighs the traffic saved.

Note that Grover, the workload the dense pass handled worst, is the one that gains most. That is not a coincidence: it is the workload with the highest proportion of cheap operators, so it had the most to gain from not densifying them.

The architectural consequence is the important part. The engine needs three paths, not one: a phase path with no shuffle at all, a permutation path with no multipliers, and the MAC array for what is genuinely dense. Sizing the MAC array for 100% of blocks would waste most of it.

04bThe other problem, which is a better fit

This assessment has pointed at the density-matrix case twice from opposite directions — the arithmetic-intensity argument in section 03, and the refill model's conclusion that the engine only wins where the state fits on chip. It is worth measuring rather than arguing.

The difference is structural. A state vector of n qubits is 2n amplitudes and a gate touches each once: the work is linear in the data, which is why this whole project has been a memory-bandwidth story. A density matrix is 4n entries and a channel is ρ → Σ K ρ K† — two matrix products per Kraus operator, so the work is O(8n) against O(4n) of data. The arithmetic per byte grows with the system instead of sitting at 0.44.

Every problem in this assessment disappears

Banking. A matrix product has regular dense access. No bit-permutation stride, so none of the coding theory in section 08 applies — it is ordinary GEMM blocking, which is solved.

Fusion, and its host cost. The operators are the Kraus set. Nothing to fuse, nothing to classify, no 116 µs per block, no cap at k = 5.

Refill. At ten qubits ρ is 16 MiB and fits on chip. Nothing spills, so none of the 20-to-44× traffic.

The three datapaths. There is one, a MAC array, and it is busy.

And here is the part that answers the original question most directly. A state-vector engine is not CUTE — it needs a shuffle fabric, three datapaths and a bank mapping derived from coding theory, none of which a convolution engine has. A density-matrix engine is a complex GEMM machine, which is very nearly CUTE already: adapt MatrixTE and ReducePE to complex arithmetic, keep ABMatrixReg, keep the Omega fabric for operand staging, and most of the design carries over unchanged.

Two honest caveats. The capacity ceiling is hard and low — eleven qubits, because 4n grows fast — so this is an engine for small noisy systems, not for scale. And if the kernel is complex GEMM, then the quantum-specific contribution is thin: much of the value would be in the surrounding compiler and verification work rather than in the datapath. That is a less exciting claim than a novel architecture, and a much more defensible one.

And it is built

ComplexGemm is the datapath: a tile × tile array of complex accumulators fed one column of A and one row of B per cycle, accumulating their outer product. After depth cycles it holds a tile of C = A · B. Verified against Scotty's own complex matrix product the same two ways as everything else — exactly against an integer model of its arithmetic, and for drift against the doubles.

Its deviation is 2.8 × 10-7 over a depth-16 product, which is no worse than the single-multiply kernels at 1.2 to 3.4. Sixteen products accumulate before one rounding, because the accumulator is widened and narrowed only on the way out. That is the property that makes a deep GEMM safe in fixed point, and it is what lets the same design run to the depth 2n a ten-qubit channel needs.

The engine

ChannelEngine sequences the datapath into something that applies a channel. For each Kraus operator it runs T = K · ρ, keeps the intermediate, runs R = T · K† — whose right operand is the conjugate of the same column, so the same storage is read with the imaginary part negated — and accumulates. That is Channel.apply, in gates.

What is verified, and at what size

All of this runs at dimension four — two qubits — because ChannelEngine holds the dimension equal to the tile. The engine as built cannot run at the ten or eleven qubits this assessment argues for: that would be a 1024-wide tile, some twenty million cells of multiplier. Reaching those sizes needs a loop over output tiles with the intermediate in a memory rather than in registers, which is control work this does not have.

So the honest statement is verified at dimension four, argued to 1024. The arithmetic generalises — the depth sweep above is the evidence for that — but the engine does not, yet.

One capability gap alongside it: Scotty's erasure channel maps dimension 2 to dimension 3, and this engine assumes ρ, K and the result are all the same square size. That is a channel pctc uses, so it is a gap on the motivating workload rather than on an exotic case.

Forty-four cycles is eleven per Kraus operator: four for each matrix product, and three for capture, accumulate and sequencing. So 73% of cycles do arithmetic at this size, and the fixed three amortise as the dimension grows — 91% at sixteen.

The cleanest number in this assessment

All four land within 6% of each other at about 20 000 cells per complex multiply-accumulate. Phase tables, Benes networks, wide accumulators — every architectural difference this page has argued over is noise against the multipliers.

Which settles the comparison. Silicon per MAC is the same whichever engine you build. What differs is how much useful work a MAC does, and that is arithmetic intensity: 0.44 flop/byte for an unfused state vector, 15.9 for the best fused block, 683 for a ten-qubit density matrix. Same cost, forty-three times the work. The density-matrix engine does not win because it is cheaper; it wins because its multipliers are busy.

05The capacity ceiling

This part is unforgiving, and no amount of silicon fixes it. A single-precision complex amplitude is 8 bytes, so the state is 2n × 8 bytes:

A hardware state-vector simulator will not beat commodity hardware on qubit count. That is a memory-capacity problem. Any pitch that leads with qubit count is dead on arrival.

06Where it could actually win

  1. Gate latency and energy per gate at small n. Not throughput on one large circuit — round-trip time in a tight CPU-to-accelerator loop. That is the regime of variational algorithms and of parameter sweeps like pctc’s CoordinateSearch and Capacities, which re-evaluate a small problem thousands of times. Serial, small-data, latency-bound: a poor GPU workload and a good tightly-coupled-accelerator workload.
  2. Density-matrix and noisy simulation, which is where this codebase already lives. A density matrix over n qubits has 4n entries, so the qubit count halves — 10 to 12 qubits fits entirely on chip — and applying a Kraus channel is a dense superoperator product with genuinely good arithmetic intensity, no fusion trickery required. This is the strongest fit between the existing work and a CUTE-shaped engine, and a less crowded field than state-vector simulation.
  3. A publishable hardware/software co-design artifact. CUTE itself is a journal paper with a repository attached. That is the genre, and it is a legitimate goal.

One thing stays in software: pctc’s Eigen, Cholesky and NullSpace on 2- to 24-dimensional matrices. Iterative, branchy, ill-conditioned, tiny. Bad silicon, fine Scala.

The refill model narrows this list. Measured at 24 qubits, an ordinary circuit that spills off chip moves 20 to 44 times the state through external memory, at which point the datapath is not the bottleneck and the engine has given away its advantage. A circuit of phases and permutations needs one pass. So the three cases above survive only in a sharper form: small enough to fit on chip, or diagonal-heavy enough to stream. The density-matrix case is the strongest precisely because it is both — 10 to 12 qubits is 4n entries that fit, and a Kraus channel is a dense superoperator applied in place.

08Roadmap

Stage 1 · in progress · Scala only

Make the software case first

No Chisel until the numbers justify it. The fusion pass, its cost model and the benchmark above are the deliverable, and they have already changed the design: the engine’s job is to be bandwidth-cheap and flop-rich, not to run a faster simulator.

  • Done: greedy fusion pass over Scotty’s circuit IR; dense lowering of the whole gate set, mirroring the simulator’s own decompositions; FusionCost roofline model; FusedKernel split out with no parallel-collections dependency so the path can be linked by Scala.js; equivalence tests against the unfused simulator across the gate set and randomised circuits; FusionBench. 197 tests pass.
  • Done: scotty.algorithm — a QFT checked against an explicitly constructed DFT matrix, Grover, and Shor’s period finding for 15 with the standard hand-compiled modular exponentiation. These are the difftest vectors for every later stage, and they are checked for the answer, not just for agreement.
  • Done: sparsity-aware lowering — the form lattice above, the native escape for gates wider than the budget, and a flop-budget knob on the pass, since whether trading traffic for arithmetic is a good deal depends on the machine. 202 tests pass.
  • Next: re-measure wall-clock on a quiet machine, then commutation-aware and look-ahead placement. What those policies should optimise has changed: it is the form mix now, not the block count.
  • A Scala.js path for the simulator. The algorithms are platform-neutral, but QuantumSimulator imports scala.collection.parallel, which is JVM-only — which is why pctc’s browser build compiles only the density-matrix sources. The fusion kernel is already clear of it; the simulator needs a sequential path or a cross-build.

Stage 2 · done · bank conflicts answered

Cycle-approximate model

The bank-conflict question turned out to have an exact answer rather than a statistical one, because of how the addresses are shaped.

Within a slice, the 2k addresses differ only in the bits at the supported qubit positions, so the address set is base + V where V is a linear subspace over GF(2) — addition is XOR, since base is zero at exactly those positions. Any bank mapping worth building is linear too: bank = L · address, a tree of XOR gates. And then bank(base + v) = bank(base) ⊕ bank(v), so the multiset of banks a slice touches is a translate of L(V) and its shape does not depend on the slice at all. Every slice conflicts identically, and

cycles per slice = 2^(k − rank of L's columns at the support)

which the model checks against counting banks directly, for every slice, rather than trusting the algebra. So the design question is a coding-theory question: a mapping gathers every support of width k in one cycle if and only if every k of its columns are linearly independent — that is, L is the parity-check matrix of a code of minimum distance k+1.

The obvious mapping never works, at any bank count. Taking the low address bits leaves every column beyond bankBits equal to zero, so a block touching a high qubit degenerates completely — all 2k of its addresses land in one bank. That is the failure the risk was about, and it is as bad as it could be.

Single-cycle gathering for every support stops being affordable past k = 3: an extended-Hamming mapping reaches it with 64 banks, and BCH needs 2048 to reach k = 5. But single-cycle gathering is not what sets throughput — the mean is. At a realistic 32 banks:

The risk resolves

Bank conflicts are real and severe — the naive mapping runs 13.6× over the floor at the k = 6 design point, and is never conflict-free at any bank count. They are also essentially solved by a handful of XOR gates: a mapping whose columns are a leading one plus five address bits gets the mean within 20% of the floor.

This was the stated go/no-go for RTL. It goes — and the two models below show the penalty is paid in buffering, issue logic, or bandwidth, depending on how much reordering you are willing to build.

The schedule, which moves the answer

That bound assumes one access per bank per cycle and no queuing, which makes it a statement about a slice. Slices are independent, though: one whose addresses pile into a single bank leaves the other thirty-one idle, and the next slice can use them. So the bound is pessimistic about throughput, and the real question is not whether a mapping works but how much buffering it costs to make it work.

This softens the headline above and sharpens it at the same time. With enough slices in flight even the naive low-bits mapping reaches full bandwidth, so its 13.6× is a latency penalty rather than a bandwidth one — it buys its way out with buffering. But buffering is the cost: depth 32 against extended Hamming's depth 8, and a slice in flight carries 64 outstanding accesses, so that is 2 048 against 512. Four times the queue to reach the same bandwidth.

What only the schedule could see

The distinct mapping is capped at half the bandwidth no matter how deep the window, because half the banks are not reachable at all. Its columns are the values 1 to 14, none of which sets the fifth bank bit, so a sixth of the address space never addresses the upper sixteen banks. No amount of queuing fixes a bank that is never asked for.

The per-slice model could not see this. It measures conflicts within a slice and reports distinct as one of the better mappings — 1.27× the floor at k = 6. A mapping has to be checked for coverage as well as for conflicts, and those are different properties.

One methodological note, because it nearly became a wrong result. The first run of this model sampled 256 of 16 384 slices and reported distinct as reaching only 16 banks — which was true of the run and false of the mapping, since a short prefix leaves the high address bits always zero and the banks they would reach never appear. The numbers above enumerate every slice. That distinct is genuinely capped is the conclusion after fixing the artifact, not before it.

The arbiter, which takes some of it back

That scheduler allowed any pending access to be served in any order — an arbiter with unlimited reordering and unlimited queues. Real hardware has neither, and the three constraints that bite are finite per-bank queues, a finite issue width, and issue that is in order or nearly so. The third is the one that matters: a blocked request stalls everything behind it while other banks sit idle, which is head-of-line blocking, and it is precisely what the optimistic model assumed away.

A correction to the correction

The previous section said the naive mapping buys its way to full bandwidth with buffering. That was true of an arbiter with unbounded queues and free reordering, and it is false under realistic issue: with depth-4 queues low bits reaches 9.7 of 32 even at a lookahead of 256.

The reason is a burst-length effect the first model could not express. Each slice sends all 2k of its accesses to one bank, so the issue window has to reach past an entire slice — 64 requests — before it finds anything for an idle bank. Queue depth barely helps: from q = 1 to q = 16 low bits moves 2.0 → 2.9, while extended Hamming moves 17.4 → 31.4.

In order, with modest queues, the bank mapping is worth 16× in bandwidth — 1.1 against 18.2. That is the number to design against, not the per-slice floor and not the optimistic schedule.

This table went round a loop

A review observed that the model's lookahead parameter did not bound what it claimed, because the issue window was refilled inside the issue loop. Correcting it by moving the refill to the cycle boundary also made lookahead the window capacity, which caps throughput at lookahead accesses per cycle — and that produced a table reading 1.0 everywhere in order and exactly 8.0 at look 8, which is the cap rather than any property of a bank mapping. A second review caught that.

The model now sizes its window to reach the issue width and bounds only the scan distance, which is what the parameter always claimed to mean. Doing so reproduces the original numbers: the first version's figures were right and its code was unclear, the correction was wrong, and the version above is both. The intermediate 9× figure, published here for part of a day, was an artifact of the fix rather than of the original.

The crossbar is the easy half

Routing amplitudes from banks to lanes turns out to be structurally constrained in a way the operator permutation is not. Lane l holds the amplitude at base + offset(l), and offset is linear in l, so

bank(l) = L(base) ⊕ L(offset(l))

is an affine function of the lane index whose linear part is just L's columns at the support. Classifying every gather over every support and slice — all 1 024 of them, not a prefix — confirms it: a gather is affine, or it conflicts and is not a permutation of lanes at all. Nothing is ever arbitrary, against 58.8% for the operator permutations the permute path has to route. The classifier carries an Arbitrary case that stays empty and a conclusion that is printed only when it does, so the claim is one the model could have refuted.

One nuance worth keeping, because the earlier version of this page got it loose. Bit permutations — what an Omega network routes natively — are almost absent here, at 0.00% to 0.90% depending on the mapping. The address stride is a bit permutation of the index, but the bank mapping XORs bits together on the way, and that is exactly what makes the gather conflict-free. So the two are coupled: the thing that fixes bank conflicts is the thing that stops the crossbar being a bit permutation. Since a full Benes network measured at 1.4% of a datapath, the resolution is to spend it and stop optimising.

The three integration questions

Those were the gaps left: a per-bank request network rather than one global issue port, contention between operator loads and amplitude traffic, and the writeback path. Two of them turn out to need arithmetic rather than simulation.

Deeper queues buy a great deal. A request network, as far as this can tell, buys nothing. Those are different claims, and two earlier versions of this page conflated them. What the sweep below varies is per-bank queue depth, with the generator strictly in order:

The shape is the burst length. A slice sends all 2k of its accesses — 64 here — to however many banks the mapping spreads it across, and an in-order generator can only reach the next slice once the current one fits. So low bits needs a queue the size of a whole slice before it overlaps slices at all: 1.3 at depth 16, then 28.5 at depth 64. A mapping that spreads is most of the way there by depth 16.

Depth is one lever and bypass is the other; the arbiter table above sweeps bypass. Both work, and they substitute for each other to a degree — extended Hamming reaches about 30 either by depth 16 with no bypass, or by depth 4 with a lookahead of 256.

A claim retracted, twice

This section twice carried a conclusion about interconnects that its experiment did not support — first that a request network buys almost nothing, then the opposite. Neither comparison isolated what it claimed. The question is posed properly below instead.

Where the buffer budget belongs

The thing those claims were groping at has a well-posed form. A design has some number of request slots to spend, and two ways to organise them: partitioned across banks, where a slot only ever serves its own bank, or pooled in a reorder window, where any slot holds any request but a blocked one occupies it until its bank drains. Those are the two classic buffer organisations and they strand storage in opposite situations.

Asking it that way needed no new model. The arbiter already is a bypassing generator with per-bank queues — occupancy is the queues, the window is the reorder buffer, lookahead is how far the generator may bypass a full bank. What was missing was a comparison at equal storage.

Two results, and the second is the one to design against

Pooled storage wins at every budget and every mapping, and structurally rather than narrowly. A bank drains one request per cycle, so a queue deeper than one buys nothing unless the generator cannot refill it — and a generator with a reorder window and the freedom to bypass always can. Depth absorbs bursts that bypass has already absorbed. The earlier instinct to reach for per-bank queues was backwards: the slots belong in the window.

What the bank mapping buys is how small that window can be. Extended Hamming reaches 31.9 of 32 on a budget of 512 slots. Low bits reaches 15.4 on the same budget, and needs 2 048 — four times the storage — to match it. That is the mapping's value stated in the currency a designer actually spends, and it is the first version of this number that rests on a comparison between two things that differ in one respect.

Operator traffic is not in contention. A block loads its operator once and then streams the entire state past it, so the only question is whether the load amortises, and the ratio answers it: a dense 4k-entry operator against 2n amplitudes is 0.39% of traffic at n = 20, k = 6, and 0.02% at n = 24, k = 6. It only stops being negligible when the state is small enough to be comparable to the operator — 50% at n = 12 — which is the regime where there is nothing to accelerate.

Writeback halves everything, and need not. Every amplitude read is transformed and written back to the same address, hence the same bank, so the traffic doubles while its distribution stays identical — the bank histogram does not change at all. Extended Hamming goes from 31.4 to 15.7. But the write has the same address as the read that preceded it, so a memory supporting read-modify-write recovers the whole factor of two. That is a memory feature rather than an arbiter one, and it is the single largest lever left in the memory path.

Refill, and what it does to the case

Everything above assumes the state is on chip. Past about twenty qubits it is not, and the engine works in tiles: load one, apply what can be applied, store it back. A block can be applied to a tile in isolation only if every qubit it touches is local — inside the address bits the tile spans. A block reaching above that line needs amplitudes from several tiles at once, so the state has to be re-tiled first, and that costs a full traversal of off-chip memory in each direction.

So above the capacity line the figure of merit is passes, not cycles. And the form lattice comes back, in its third role: a diagonal block streams. It scales each amplitude independently, so it can be applied to a tile whatever its support — including a support spanning every qubit in the register. Grover's all-qubit controlled Z, which forced a native escape in the fusion pass and looked like the worst case there, is free here. Monomial and dense blocks move amplitudes between lanes and do need their support local.

This narrows where the engine wins

An ordinary circuit above the capacity line moves 20 to 44 times the state through off-chip memory. At that point the on-chip datapath is not the bottleneck and none of the work above matters much: the engine's whole advantage is that its traffic stays on chip, and spilling gives it away.

Capacity helps less than one would hope. Going from 16 local qubits to 22 — sixty-four times the on-chip store — takes brickwork only from 14 passes to 10, because what forces a re-tiling is the circuit's structure, blocks reaching across the register, not the shortfall in room. Scheduling helps more than capacity does: a 64-block lookahead nearly halves the passes.

And a circuit of phases and permutations needs one pass. That is not a detail; it is the shape of the answer. The engine is for circuits that fit, or for circuits that are mostly diagonal.

Overlapping the re-tiling

Those passes need not be stalls, and there are two distinct ways to hide them. Only one is interesting.

Double buffering prefetches the next tile while computing on the current one, turning a sum into a maximum. Worth 10% to 47% here depending on the bandwidth ratio — useful, unremarkable.

Folding is the one that matters. A re-tiling already streams every amplitude past the engine, so a block can be applied as the data goes by rather than queueing behind it. How much work a pass can carry is set by the ratio of on-chip to off-chip bandwidth: while off-chip delivers one amplitude, on-chip can do many blocks' arithmetic on it.

The uncomfortable version of a good result

While off-chip delivers at most about an eighth of the on-chip rate, a circuit's entire computation folds into its transfer passes. The cost of a spilled circuit is then exactly its pass count, and nothing else — not the fusion width, not the bank mapping, not the datapath this page spent its length on. Above the capacity line the engine should be designed to minimise re-tilings, and its arithmetic is free.

That is a real answer to “is the traffic a wall or a background cost”: it is a wall, and computation hides behind it completely.

One caution against reading the regime as settled. The 32-amplitudes-per-cycle on-chip figure is this project's banking model, which chose 32 banks; a wider store would raise it. And off-chip is not obviously slow — HBM at a few terabytes per second is, at a gigahertz, well over a hundred fp64 complex amplitudes per cycle, which is the other side of the table entirely. Where a real part sits depends on a bank count that is a design choice and a memory technology that is not. What the model establishes is the crossover, not which side of it to expect.

What the host costs

Every block has to be built before it can be applied: gates fused into one operator, its form classified, a Benes routing computed if it is monomial, strides worked out. That runs on the CPU, and if it is slower than the engine then none of the datapath matters — the engine starves.

The asymmetry is not obviously in the engine's favour. Applying a block touches every amplitude once, so its cost grows with the state; building a dense one is a matrix product per gate fused, O(8k), which grows with the block and not with the state at all.

A third constraint on the fusion width, and the tightest one

Section 03 argued for k = 6 to 8 from arithmetic intensity, bounded above by operator storage. The host adds a third bound and it binds first: at twenty qubits, k = 6 is already host-bound, and the engine would wait 1.78 cycles for every one it works.

The crossover moves with the register, because engine time grows as 2n while host time does not: k = 6 becomes engine-bound at n ≥ 21. But twenty qubits is about where a 16 MiB on-chip store runs out, so the sizes at which the engine is host-bound are exactly the sizes at which it fits on chip — which the refill model says is the only place it wins. The two constraints close on the same point from opposite sides.

The way out is modest: design at k = 5. It is comfortably engine-bound at 0.37×, still gives 6.7 flop/byte and a 16 KiB operator, and the measurements elsewhere on this page say intensity was never the binding constraint anyway.

Caching does not rescue k = 6. A repeated operator need only be built once, and real circuits do repeat — but measured reuse is 1.2× on the brickwork circuit, 3.1× on Grover and 1.1× on the diagonal-heavy one. Grover repeats its oracle; a brickwork layer varies its angles and so repeats almost nothing.

Two caveats, both pointing the same way. This times this fusion pass, written for clarity in Scala 3 and never optimised; a tuned implementation in a systems language could plausibly move the crossover by several times. And it assumes one core preparing for one engine, where a real host would use several. The structure of the finding would survive both — host cost is flat in the state and the engine's is not — but the particular crossover point would not.

Stage 3 · begun · one block standing

RTL

All three datapaths exist and are verified against Scotty, every test running under Verilator. The two Scala versions meet through a generated vector file, for the reason given under verification below.

  • AddressGen — the bit insertion that turns a slice number into a base amplitude address. Combinational, one stage per supported qubit, checked against all 592 exhaustive vectors for six qubits and supports up to three wide.
  • MonomialKernel — the permute path: one complex multiplier per lane and a Benes network, a whole slice per cycle. Not an Omega network, and that is a measured decision: fusing randomised circuits and classifying the permutations that come out gives 7.7% bit permutations, 33.5% affine over GF(2) and 58.8% arbitrary — Toffoli and Fredkin generate permutations that are not affine, so an Omega network, which is blocking, could not route most blocks. A Benes network routes every permutation in (2k−1)·2k−1 switches, with the settings computed by the host once per block. The vectors used here contain an arbitrary permutation, so the test exercises exactly the case Omega would fail.
  • DenseKernel — the MAC array: a full 2^k × 2^k complex matrix-vector product, one output row per cycle. Checked the same two ways, and additionally simulated under Verilator against the same vectors, which is what says the generated Verilog agrees with the elaborated design rather than merely that the design is right. Its deviation from Scotty is 1.06 × 10-7, lower than the diagonal kernel's despite accumulating eight products, because the accumulator is widened and only the final shift rounds.
  • DiagonalKernel — the phase path: a 2^k phase table, one complex multiplier, one amplitude per cycle. The first block that is not bit-exact, so it is checked twice: against an exact integer model of its own fixed-point arithmetic, which admits no tolerance; and against Scotty's doubles, which is how the precision number above was obtained. In hardware one complex multiplier is four real ones — against the dense path's 2^k-wide MAC array, that is the whole argument for keeping the forms apart.

09Verification strategy

This is the part the existing codebase makes cheap, and it is why Scotty is the right starting point rather than an incidental one.

  • Scotty is the golden model. 3.7k lines of Scala 3, 172 passing tests, amplitudes already in the interleaved (re, im, re, im) flat-array layout that hardware wants.
  • The fusion pass is checked against the simulator, not against a second reading of the gate definitions. FusedBlock.lower deliberately mirrors QuantumSimulator.applyGate case for case and reuses its composite decompositions, so the lowering is the simulator’s semantics by construction.
  • Bit conventions are pinned by test. Qubit 0 is the least significant bit of the amplitude index; local bit j of a block operator is global qubit qubits(j). There is a characterisation test for this, because every dense operator in the pass is transposed into the wrong basis if it ever flips.
  • Algorithm-level vectors, which now exist. Grover and Shor give end-to-end checks with known answers — a marked amplitude that must approach 1, a period that must come back as 4, a factorisation that must be 3 × 5 — rather than amplitude diffs alone. A diff only tells you two implementations agree; these tell you the implementation is right. Both are asserted at every fusion width, so a pass that preserved norm and phase relationships but broke the algorithm would still be caught.
  • ChiselSim works, but only on Chisel 7. On 6.7.0 — the version CUTE pins — ChiselSim's svsim desynchronises with Unexpected message: Ready on the first clock edge against Verilator 5.052, on a design as trivial as AddressGen; and chiseltest, the alternative, stopped at 6.0.0 and its Verilator backend fails to compile its own C++ harness with unknown type name 'WData'. On Chisel 7.0.0-RC1 the same ChiselSim test passes. So the project is on Chisel 7 and every test now runs under Verilator rather than an interpreter. Reusing CUTE's modules later will mean matching whatever Chisel it is on then; until there is a module to reuse, a working simulator is worth more.

    A SystemVerilog testbench driving Verilator through $readmemh is kept alongside, for the dense kernel. It tests the generated netlist with the toolchain an FPGA bring-up would use, which is a slightly different question from whether the elaborated design is right.
  • Scala 2.13 consuming Scala 3: tried, and it does not work. CUTE sets -Ytasty-reader, which in principle lets Chisel code call Scotty’s jar directly, so it was worth testing rather than assuming. 2.13.15 resolves the package and then fails on every type with class file Complex.tasty is broken — Scotty’s 3.9 TASTy is simply newer than that reader understands. So the golden model feeds the testbench through generated vectors, which is the better arrangement regardless: it is the same mechanism a C++ difftest harness or an FPGA bring-up would use, and it is how CuteDifftest and CUTEFPE appear to work.

10Open risks

  • The exchange rate may never close. If an achievable MAC array cannot beat 8·2k/14k flops per k× of bandwidth saved, there is no engine worth building. Stage 2 exists to answer this before any RTL.
  • The mix is measured on two workloads, not derived. The 2:2:3 split the diagram is annotated with comes from a brickwork circuit and Grover. It is enough to establish that the dense path is the minority — which is the architectural point — but not enough to size three datapaths against each other. The area cost of the two cheap paths is still unestimated, and a wider workload survey should come before anyone commits silicon to a ratio.
  • Bank conflicts: answered, and the answer is a bank mapping rather than a fabric. Both shuffle problems that were hiding under one word now have measurements. The operator's permutation is usually arbitrary and needs a Benes network, which costs 1.4% of its datapath. The memory-side question is settled by stage 2: under in-order issue with modest queues the bank mapping is worth 16× in bandwidth, 1.1 accesses per cycle against 18.2 of a possible 32, and the buffer budget around it matters a great deal, and belongs in a pooled reorder window rather than in per-bank queues: a bank drains one request per cycle, so depth beyond one is redundant with the bypass a window already provides. At equal storage the bank mapping is worth four times the budget — extended Hamming reaches 31.9 of 32 on 512 slots where low bits needs 2 048. What is not modelled is refill from a level below when the state does not fit on chip, and the CPU-side cost of preparing each block's descriptors.
  • A bank mapping has to be checked for coverage, not only for conflicts. One of the candidates reaches only half the banks for some supports, capping bandwidth at 50% however deep the queue, and the per-slice model rated it among the better ones. The two properties are different and only the schedule distinguishes them.
  • Operator construction on the critical path. Fusion costs O(8k) per gate on the CPU side. For a latency-bound loop — the main motivation — that cost may eat the gain. Caching operators across sweeps with only the parameters changed is the obvious mitigation and is untested.
  • Precision — now with one data point. Everything in the simulator is fp64 complex. The first RTL to do arithmetic, the diagonal kernel, uses Q1.23 fixed point instead, and the cost of that was measured rather than assumed: the worst deviation from Scotty is 3.3 × 10-7 for the diagonal path, 3.4 for the dense and 1.2 for the permute, a handful of quantisation steps each. Fixed point is clearly not disqualifying, which was the open question. It remains three blocks on three circuits, not a study — error accumulates over a circuit's depth and nothing here measures that.

    The format itself took a correction. Q1.23 looked natural, since amplitudes live in the unit disc, but it cannot represent 1.0 — the range stops at 1 − 2−23, so a coefficient of exactly 1 wraps to −1. Unitary operators are full of ±1: every X, CNOT, SWAP and Toffoli is a permutation matrix of them. The exact-model check caught it as a sign flip on the permute path; the other two kernels had the same latent bug and had merely been given vectors that avoided unit entries. Everything is Q2.22 now, one bit of precision for correctness.
  • A latent inconsistency in Scotty. registerToState tensors qubit 0 in as the most significant factor, while applyTargetGate treats qubit 0 as the least significant bit. For an all-zero or all-one register this is invisible, which is why it has survived; for a custom register it is not. The fusion pass matches the simulator either way, so it is unaffected, but it should be resolved before Scotty is used as a golden model for anything outside it.
